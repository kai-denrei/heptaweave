Ring Stave Numerals

The ring is the stave. Four short stems leave it at right angles, and the Cistercian digit logic hangs off each stem unchanged.

In a Cistercian numeral the four digits hang off one vertical stave, one per quadrant, and every digit shape is a small figure attached to that stave. Here the stave bends into the heptaweave’s ring. At four points the ring throws out a stem, radial and short, and the digit is drawn in the stem’s own frame: along the stem is what “up the stave” used to be, across it is what “out from the stave” used to be.

Nothing about the digit shapes changes. 1 and 2 are bars, 3 and 4 are diagonals, 5 closes into a triangle, 6 stands apart, 7 and 8 are bowls, 9 is a square. What changes is where a stave lives: it now sprouts from a circle, four times.

The ten shapes, on one stem

Stem frame: the stem rises from the ring (foot) to its tip. A digit uses two levels, the tip T and a foot level F a little above the ring, and one width w to the digit side. Zero is a bare stem.

Four places, four stems

How it is written in ink

Same growth front as the Cistercian prompt, with the ring already on the water. The front starts where each stem meets the ring and runs outward: stem first, then the digit figure, all four places at once. Digit 6 keeps its own seed, exactly as it does today. In the counter, a changed digit unpins and dissolves while the new one grows from the ring; the ring itself is never redrawn.

To settle before building

  • Stem positions. The diagonals (45°, 135°, 225°, 315°) as drawn, or the four lobe positions the heptaweave already uses for its Morse marks? The lobes keep the two glyph systems on one skeleton; the diagonals keep the Cistercian quadrant reading cleaner.
  • Inside or outside. Stems shooting outward (drawn) grow the figure; stems pointing inward keep it inside the ring and read more like a seal. Outward is the recommendation: the ring stays a ring, and the counter’s pinned ring has empty water around it to grow into.
  • Stem length and digit width. Drawn at 0.42 R for the stem and 0.6 of that for w. A shorter stem reads as “ring with ticks”; longer stops reading as one figure. Tune on the phone.
  • Mirroring for the lower places. Full Cistercian symmetry (tip/foot swap) as drawn, or side-mirroring only. Full symmetry is faithful to the source; side-only is easier to learn.
  • The gap and the wet-drop. Keep the enso’s opening and drop? They would sit between stems. The counter’s ring already carries them; the glyph could inherit both for free.
